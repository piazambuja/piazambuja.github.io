# LinkedIn profile for recruiters

Goal: a profile that a recruiter for the user's target role finds in search, understands in 10 seconds and wants to contact. Specific beats impressive. When a CV exists, profile and CV must tell the same story: recruiters who receive a referral open both and compare.

Works for any field and seniority. The examples below come from different areas on purpose; adapt them to the user's own field, market and language.

## Hard rules

- **Never invent facts.** No made-up metrics, employers, titles, dates, tools, certifications or results. When a bullet needs a number the user didn't give, write a placeholder like `[X% reduction in onboarding time]` and ask for it. If the user has no number, write the bullet without one.
- No hype words ("top 1%", "ninja", "rockstar", "guru", "passionate about", "exceptional results", and their equivalents in other languages). Show it with specifics instead.
- Write in the language of the profile (default: the user's language). Keep the terms the user's market uses as-is, even when they're in English.
- Respect LinkedIn limits: headline 220 characters, About 2,600 characters (about the first 265 show before "see more"), experience description 2,000 characters.
- Don't add claims about leaving a job, layoffs or salary unless the user brings it up. When they do, use the transition mode below.
- Personal data the user shares (phone, email, dates) goes only into their own profile and CV.

## Step 1: gather (ask only what's missing, in one message)

1. The current profile: headline, About, experience, education, certifications, skills. Pasted text, the LinkedIn PDF export, a browser session on the profile, or the CV all work. Ask for both CV and profile when the user has both.
2. Target role(s) and seniority, e.g. "Senior Product Designer", "Financial Controller", "Staff Backend Engineer".
3. Target market: country, remote/hybrid/on-site, employment type (permanent, contract, freelance), company types or specific companies.
4. Two or three real results per recent role, with numbers if they exist: revenue, cost, time saved, quality, customers or users served, team size, volume handled.
5. **Recent work that isn't written anywhere yet.** Ask directly: "What did you work on in the last 6–12 months that isn't in your CV or profile?" The newest work is often missing from both, and it's frequently the strongest differentiator.

## Step 2: CV × LinkedIn alignment (when both exist)

Read both end to end before proposing any text. Build a divergence table:

| Item | LinkedIn | CV |
|---|---|---|

Check at least: job titles, start and end dates of every role, employer names, education (institution, course, status: completed / incomplete / in progress), city, contact details, certifications and their validity.

- Ask which version is correct for each row, in one message. Don't pick a side silently.
- Use public evidence to flag a likely error, and ask about it: a post announcing a promotion, a certificate page showing an expiry date. Example: "Your post from May announces the promotion, but both versions say September. Is September right?"
- Flag what a recruiter would question: a role still shown as current after the user left; an expired certification listed as valid (show its period, e.g. "PMP (2019–2022)", or suggest renewing); incomplete education hidden instead of shown as incomplete; mixed date formats ("Sep 2021", "sep/21", "1 Sep 2021": use one format, `MM/YYYY`, no day).
- Show incomplete education honestly ("incomplete") next to a current course ("in progress"). It explains the user's background and reads better than a gap.
- End this step with two lists: what changes on LinkedIn, and what changes in the CV.

## Step 3: recruiter test (diagnosis first)

Act as an experienced recruiter hiring for the target role (at the target company, if given). Answer honestly:
- Would you reach out? Yes / maybe / no, and why, in two sentences.
- The 5 biggest problems, ordered by impact. Typical ones: headline is only a job title; About opens with "I am a dedicated professional…"; experience lists duties instead of results; target keywords missing; recent work missing; no proof (Featured empty); a gap or title that needs context.
- What a recruiter would search for, and whether this profile appears.

**Don't rewrite what already works.** If a section of the current profile is stronger than what you'd draft, say so and keep it; propose only targeted edits. Show the diagnosis before rewriting, so the user can correct wrong assumptions.

## Step 4: rewrite

**Headline (10 variations).** Formula: role + specialty + proof or domain + (optional) what you want next. Mix styles: keyword-dense for search, outcome-led, domain-led. Example shapes from different fields:
- `Senior Product Designer | Design Systems & Accessibility | B2B SaaS`
- `Financial Controller | IFRS, Month-End Close & ERP Migrations | Retail`
- `Registered Nurse | ICU & Emergency Care | Clinical Training`

If the user's market recruits in more than one language (e.g. English-speaking recruiters searching a non-English market), offer the recommended headline in both. Recommend one and say why.

**About (under 2,000 characters).**
- The first two lines must work alone in the preview: who you are for the target role plus the strongest proof.
- Then a short problem → approach → result arc that stays factual: the problem you solve for employers, how you approach it, 2–3 concrete results.
- If the user's most recent work is their differentiator, it gets its own paragraph near the top and the first item in any list.
- A line with core skills, tools or keywords, as prose or a short list.
- A clear call to action: what you're open to and how to reach you.

**Experience (per role).** Bullets in the XYZ shape, "Accomplished X, measured by Y, by doing Z", led by a strong verb (reduced, built, launched, led, negotiated, automated, trained). 3–5 bullets per recent role, 1–2 for older ones. Put the most recent and most relevant work first in the current role. Placeholders where a number is missing. A one-line role summary above the bullets for context (team, scale, domain). Several roles at one employer go under one company entry, so LinkedIn shows the progression.

**Name the market category.** Translate what the user describes into the terms recruiters search for, and use those terms in headline, About and skills. Examples:
- "I built the spreadsheet everyone now uses to plan stock" → demand planning, inventory forecasting.
- "I made every team request cloud/vendor spend through one process so we could bill it back" → cost governance, chargeback.
- "I train the new hires on the ward" → clinical education, preceptorship.

**Skills and keywords.** List the 25 keywords recruiters search for this role, mark each as present / missing in the profile, and say where to add the missing ones naturally (headline, About, a specific role, Skills section). Recommend the top 3 skills to pin and link new skills to the role where they were used.

LinkedIn only accepts skills from its own list, and in a non-English interface the names are often translated. Some terms don't exist at all. Suggest the closest accepted name, tell the user what didn't fit, and put the missing term in the About or experience text instead.

**Featured (5 items).** Proof over decoration: a portfolio, project or case study, an article or talk, a sanitised example of real work, certifications only if relevant, a personal site. For each: what it should contain and why it builds credibility for the target role.

**Recommendations.** Suggest asking 2–3 former managers, peers or clients now, while the work is fresh, starting with people who already refer the user.

## Transition mode (the user left or is leaving a job)

Use only when the user says so. Then also cover:
- End the last role with its real end date. A role still marked current after a public departure confuses recruiters.
- **Open to Work: "Recruiters only"** by default. It puts the profile in recruiter searches without the public green frame; mention the public option and let the user choose. Ask about job titles, start date, employment types and work model. Contract or freelance roles are common in some fields and countries: ask whether to include them. LinkedIn only accepts job titles from its list, so say which ones didn't fit.
- One availability line at the end of the About: target roles and contact.
- A short post announcing availability: what happened in one neutral line, the journey and 2–3 highlights, the roles and work model wanted, thanks to people already referring. Ask before publishing; the user may prefer to post it themselves. If a colleague's post about the user is already circulating, the user's own post can come later and focus on their expertise instead.
- Activity helps visibility: suggest one post about the user's strongest recent work, with nothing confidential.

## Step 5: updated CV in the ATS-friendly format (when asked, or after alignment)

When the user wants the CV to match the profile, apply every confirmed change from Step 2 and the rewritten content from Step 4, and lay it out in this format. It is built to be parsed by applicant tracking systems (ATS) and by LinkedIn's CV import. If the user's current CV uses a different layout (columns, sidebar, photo, icons, skill bars), say which parts an ATS may misread and convert it, unless the user explicitly asks to keep their design.

**Layout**
- One column, one page (two only for long careers). No photo, icons, tables, text boxes, columns or skill-level graphics. Plain bullets.
- Selectable text in reading order (check with a text extraction such as `pdftotext`: the output must read top to bottom in the same order as the page).
- Standard fonts, one accent colour at most, section titles set apart by a thin rule.

**Structure, in this order** (section names translated to the CV's language, e.g. Experiência Profissional / Formação / Competências):
1. **Header:** full name; the target role on the line below; one contact line with `•` separators: city, phone, email, LinkedIn URL (and a portfolio link if relevant).
2. **Work Experience**, most recent first. For each recent role:
   - `Company, City` on the left and `MM/YYYY – MM/YYYY` (or "Present") on the right.
   - One italic line describing the company: sector, size or scale (e.g. "Retail chain with 120 stores").
   - The job title in bold.
   - 3–4 bullets in the XYZ shape from Step 4, with numbers or placeholders.
   - Several titles at one employer: repeat the title line with its own dates and bullets under the same company.
   - Older or less relevant roles: one line each, `Title, Company, City` with dates on the right, no bullets.
3. **Education:** `Institution, City` with the date on the right; the degree on the line below. Show incomplete or in-progress courses as such.
4. **Skills:** grouped on labelled lines, e.g. `Hard Skills: …` and `Techniques: …` (or `Tools:`, `Languages:`), with the level in parentheses where it helps ("Spanish (Advanced)"). Use the keywords from Step 4.
5. **Certifications** only if relevant to the target role, one line each with issuer and date or validity period.

Leave out: a summary or objective paragraph (the target role under the name does that job), a soft-skills list, references, photo, date of birth and other personal data the role doesn't need.

**File**
- Keep the file small: LinkedIn accepts DOC, DOCX and PDF up to 5 MB, and some upload paths are much stricter. Standard PDF fonts (e.g. Helvetica) give a file of a few KB; embedded fonts can make it many times larger.
- Render a preview of each page and look at it before delivering.

## Editing the profile directly (when browser tools are available)

The user logs in themselves. Never type or ask for a password.
- Show the final text of each section and get an ok before saving it. Edit one section at a time and re-read it after saving.
- Turn off the option to notify the network before saving each edit, and decline LinkedIn's offers to publish a post about the change. Nothing is published without an explicit request.
- Before any delete (an old CV, a position, a skill): re-read the page, confirm the exact name of the item next to the button you'll click, and list the items again afterwards. Element references go stale when the page changes, so look them up again right before each click.
- Report what changed at the end, including choices the user may want to revisit.

## Step 6: outreach (optional, when asked)

Three short DM templates for recruiters or hiring managers at target companies, under 300 characters for connection notes and under about 600 for InMail:
- Reference something specific about the company or role.
- One line of proof that matches it.
- A low-friction ask (a 15-minute call, or "is this team hiring for X?").
No "hope you're doing well", no generic flattery.

## Output format

1. CV × LinkedIn divergence table and questions (step 2), when both exist.
2. Diagnosis (step 3).
3. Headline options with the recommended one.
4. New About, ready to paste, with character count.
5. Experience rewrites per role, ready to paste.
6. Keyword table and where to put each missing keyword.
7. Featured plan.
8. Transition items (Open to Work, availability line, post), when in transition mode.
9. What to change in the CV, or the updated CV file in the Step 5 format.
10. Placeholders and open questions the user still needs to answer, as a checklist.
11. DMs, if requested.

After the user applies the changes, offer to re-run the recruiter test on the new version. To evaluate job postings or fill in applications, use the `job-application` skill.
